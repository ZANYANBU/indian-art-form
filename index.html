What comes from where

The two traditions

Warli and Kalamkari, side by side

Similar in spirit, opposite in method: one reduces the world to triangles, the other fills it with flowers.

Warli

Palghar, Dahanu & Thane · Maharashtra

A tribal wall-painting tradition of the Warli Adivasi community, painted by women during weddings and harvests on mud walls coated with cow dung and red earth. Rice paste is the only pigment; a chewed bamboo twig the only brush.

  • Vocabulary: circle (sun, moon), triangle (mountain, tree), square (sacred enclosure); a person is two triangles joined at the waist.
  • Themes: the tarpa dance, farming, hunting, weddings, the goddess Palghat.
  • Feel: rhythmic, communal, monochrome, humble.

Kalamkari

Srikalahasti & Machilipatnam · Andhra Pradesh

A hand-painted and block-printed textile art. The name means "pen work": outlines are drawn with a bamboo kalam dipped in fermented jaggery and iron, then filled with natural dyes such as madder red, indigo and myrobalan yellow, with up to 23 steps of washing and dyeing.

  • Vocabulary: tree of life, lotus and rosette flowers, paisley (mango), peacocks and parrots, vine borders.
  • Themes: Ramayana and Mahabharata scrolls for temples, Persian-influenced florals for trade cloth.
  • Feel: lush, detailed, earthy reds and blues, flowing line.

How the fusion was built

Four steps to a confluence

  1. 01

    Study the grammar

    We listed the smallest units of each style: Warli's circle-triangle-line figures and the tarpa spiral; Kalamkari's pen outline, rosette flowers, paisley and vine border.

  2. 02

    Choose a structure

    Kalamkari gives the frame: a central medallion and a four-sided vine border, as on a temple cloth. Warli gives the movement: a ring of dancers and narrative bands of village life.

  3. 03

    Reconcile the palettes

    Warli is white on earth; Kalamkari is maroon, indigo and mustard on cream. We kept Warli figures monochrome and let Kalamkari carry all the colour, so each tradition stays recognisable.

  4. 04

    Draw it as code

    Every element is drawn with JavaScript into an SVG, so the dancers can be regenerated, layers toggled, and the piece exported at print size. Digital tools stand in for the twig and the kalam.

Project description

Title: Sangam (Confluence): a Warli × Kalamkari digital fusion artwork.

Objective (CO2): To explore the similarities and differences between two regional painting styles of India and to create a new artwork through creative synthesis, using digital tools.

Concept: Warli painting from Maharashtra and Kalamkari from Andhra Pradesh sit at opposite ends of India's visual vocabulary. Warli is minimal and geometric: white rice-paste figures built from circles, triangles and lines, arranged in rhythmic scenes of dance, farming and ritual on a red-earth wall. Kalamkari is maximal and organic: hand-drawn pen outlines filled with natural dyes, dense with flowers, vines, birds and the tree of life, and always contained within an ornamental border. Our artwork brings the two into a single composition where each keeps its own identity while completing the other.

Composition: The structure is borrowed from a Kalamkari temple cloth: a scalloped central medallion holding a tree of life with rosette flowers and a pair of birds, enclosed by a four-sided vine border with paisleys in the corners. Inside this frame the Warli world moves. A circle of dancers performs the tarpa dance around the medallion, led by a tarpa player. A band above shows the sun, moon, trees, huts and birds of a Warli village; a band below shows farmers, deer, peacocks and cattle. Vertical Kalamkari vines on the left and right complete the frame.

Colour: To keep the traditions legible, the Warli elements remain monochrome (white on terracotta, or terracotta on ivory), while all colour in the piece belongs to Kalamkari: madder maroon, indigo blue, myrobalan mustard and leaf green, outlined in kalam black. Three grounds are offered: terracotta (the Warli wall), ivory (the Kalamkari cloth) and indigo (the dye vat).

Tools and technique: The artwork is produced entirely with digital tools. Each motif is described as a small drawing function in JavaScript and rendered as scalable vector graphics in the browser, so the piece can be regenerated with a different arrangement of dancers, viewed layer by layer, and exported as a print-resolution PNG or an editable SVG. A subtle texture filter recalls the grain of cloth and mud wall.

Learning outcome: Building the piece made the contrast between the two grammars concrete. Warli's figures are easy to generate but hard to give life; Kalamkari's motifs are complex but naturally decorative. The fusion works because Warli supplies rhythm and narrative while Kalamkari supplies frame and colour, mirroring the way folk art and textile art have always borrowed from each other across regions.

For the viva / presentation

Presentation points

1 · Title

  • Sangam: a Warli × Kalamkari digital fusion.
  • Regional Painting Fusion Project, CO2, 21LEM301T Indian Art Form.
  • Team members and register numbers.

2 · Objective

  • Compare two regional styles: their origin, tools, colours and motifs.
  • Create one artwork that synthesises both without losing either identity.
  • Use digital tools as the medium.

3 · Warli in 60 seconds

  • Adivasi wall painting, Palghar–Dahanu, Maharashtra; painted by women for weddings and harvests.
  • Rice paste on cow-dung and red-earth wall; bamboo twig brush.
  • Grammar: circle, triangle, line; human = two triangles.
  • Key motif: tarpa dance spiral. Key artist: Jivya Soma Mashe (Padma Shri 2011).

4 · Kalamkari in 60 seconds

  • Hand-painted (Srikalahasti) and block-printed (Machilipatnam) cloth art, Andhra Pradesh.
  • Kalam pen with jaggery-iron ink; natural dyes: madder, indigo, myrobalan; up to 23 steps.
  • Motifs: tree of life, lotus, paisley, peacock, vine borders; epic narratives for temples.
  • GI tag 2013.

5 · Similarities and differences

  • Both: natural materials, ritual origins, community-based, now commercial folk arts with legal protection.
  • Warli: geometric, monochrome, open space. Kalamkari: organic, polychrome, filled and bordered.

6 · Our fusion strategy

  • Kalamkari provides the frame: medallion, tree of life, vine border, corner paisleys.
  • Warli provides the movement: dancer ring, village band, farming band.
  • Colour rule: Warli stays monochrome; Kalamkari owns the colour.

7 · Digital process

  • Motifs written as drawing functions in JavaScript, rendered as SVG.
  • Layer toggles show each tradition alone or combined; regenerate button reshuffles dancers.
  • Export at 2400 px PNG or editable SVG; three palettes.

8 · Live demo

  • Toggle Warli layers off: a Kalamkari cloth remains.
  • Toggle Kalamkari off: a Warli wall remains.
  • Switch palettes; regenerate the dance.

9 · What we learned

  • Minimal forms need rhythm to come alive; rich forms need a frame to stay calm.
  • Fusion works when each style takes a distinct job in the composition.
  • Folk arts have always borrowed across regions; digital tools make that borrowing visible and repeatable.

10 · Conclusion and credits

  • One canvas, two traditions, both intact.
  • References: Jivya Soma Mashe retrospective, Warli; Srikalahasti Kalamkari GI documentation; museum collections.
